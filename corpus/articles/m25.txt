Title: Kidney Stones
Specialty: Nephrology
Code: N20
Updated: 2024

## Overview
Kidney stones are hard crystals that form in the kidney from substances in the urine, mainly calcium oxalate. They form when urine is concentrated, when it contains too much of a stone-forming substance, or when it lacks substances that stop crystals from sticking together. Dehydration, high salt intake, obesity, gout and some family conditions raise the risk. Stones can stay in the kidney or move down the ureter, the tube to the bladder.

## Symptoms
A stone that moves into the ureter causes sudden, severe pain in the side or back, which often spreads toward the groin. The pain may come in waves. Blood in the urine, nausea, vomiting, and a burning feeling when passing urine are also common. Fever with pain is an emergency because it can mean infection.

## Diagnosis
A urine test checks for blood, infection and crystals. A CT scan without contrast is the most accurate test for finding a stone and its size. An ultrasound scan is used in pregnancy and for children. Blood tests check kidney function and calcium levels.

## Treatment
Small stones often pass on their own with pain relief and plenty of fluids. Larger or blocked stones may be broken up with shock waves, removed with a flexible telescope, or taken out by keyhole surgery. Prevention includes drinking enough water to produce about two litres of urine a day, eating less salt and, for some patients, medicines that change the urine.
